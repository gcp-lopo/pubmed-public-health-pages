BACKGROUND: Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) are standard first-line therapy for psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Observational studies suggest earlier initiation is associated with superior minimal disease activity (MDA) attainment, yet the economic and health implications of treatment timing remain uncertain. This cost-utility analysis evaluated the cost-effectiveness of early versus delayed DMARD initiation in DMARD-naive adults with PsA from a U.S. payer perspective.

METHODS: We built a cohort state-transition (Markov) model (monthly cycles, 15-year horizon) from published data (2000-2025). Patients initiated a DMARD under two strategies: early (≤1 year post-diagnosis) and delayed initiation (>1 year). Patients remained in the pre-treatment state for six months prior to response assignment, then transitioned among complete response (sustained MDA), partial response (non-sustained MDA), non-response, no treatment, and death. Primary outcomes included the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and incremental net monetary benefit (INMB). Uncertainty intervals (UIs) were derived from probabilistic sensitivity analyses.

FINDINGS: Early DMARD initiation provided an additional 0.56 (95% UI, 0.32-0.83) QALYs at an incremental cost of -US$43,902.94 (95% UI, -US$147,096.29 to US$61,615.86) per patient relative to delayed initiation, yielding a favourable ICER (-US$78,398.11/QALY). At a willingness-to-pay threshold of US$50,000/QALY, mean INMB was US$71,902.94 (95% UI, -US$36,663.51 to US$176,658.91), with early initiation preferred in 90.6% of simulations.

INTERPRETATION: Early DMARD initiation was associated with improved long-term health outcomes at reduced costs, suggesting timely DMARD initiation may represent a high-value strategy. Given the observational basis of the key treatment-timing input, findings support disease-duration stratified analyses of existing phase III trials and further characterization of access delays.

High-dimensional near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy datasets are characterized by strong wavelength correlation, limited sample sizes, and structured experimental variability, creating significant challenges for multivariate calibration. Under these conditions, extreme multicollinearity can lead to unstable regression coefficients, inflated estimator variance, and reduced model robustness. Although numerous calibration algorithms have been proposed, comparatively little attention has been devoted to understanding how multicollinearity influences calibration behaviour across different modelling paradigms. This study systematically investigates the performance of classical chemometric, regularized linear, dimensionality reduction, wavelength selection, and nonlinear machine learning approaches using a publicly available glucose NIR spectroscopy dataset comprising 4200 wavelength variables and structured experimental perturbations. Partial Least Squares (PLS), Ridge Regression, Elastic Net, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Genetic Algorithm-based PLS (GA-PLS), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Gaussian Process Regression were evaluated using repeated cross-validation and independent validation. Model assessment was complemented by multicollinearity diagnostics, residual analysis, and wavelength-selection stability analysis to investigate the mechanisms governing calibration robustness. SNV-PLS achieved the best overall predictive performance on the independent validation dataset (RMSE = 5.75 mM; R2 = 0.864), followed by GA-PLS (RMSE = 5.82 mM; R2 = 0.861) and SNV-Ridge (RMSE = 5.84 mM; R2 = 0.860), while SNV-Elastic Net achieved comparable performance (RMSE = 5.93 mM; R2 = 0.856). GA-PLS reduced the spectral dimensionality from 4200 to 50 wavelengths; however, repeated GA runs showed only 0-2% exact wavelength overlap, increasing to 17-29% within ±5 nm and 30-47% within ±10 nm, indicating regional rather than exact-wavelength stability. Variance Inflation Factor analysis revealed extreme predictor collinearity, while residual and domain-coverage analysis showed that the major validation deterioration was localized at 50 mM glucose, where the six validation samples were systematically underpredicted and originated from an experimental condition absent from the calibration set. The findings suggest that effective management of covariance structure and estimator variance may be more influential than increasing model complexity for the investigated high-dimensional NIR calibration problem. These results provide practical guidance for selecting robust and interpretable calibration strategies for high-dimensional spectroscopic applications affected by severe multicollinearity.

OBJECTIVES: This phase 3, randomized, double-blind, active-controlled, non-inferiority trial compared test and control varicella vaccines (VarVs) in healthy participants aged ≥13 years without prior varicella vaccination or disease.

METHODS: Conducted at a single center in China (July 2023-March 2024), 1,600 eligible participants were stratified by age (13-17 vs. ≥18 years) and randomized 1:1:1:1 to receive test or control VarV on 0- and 4-week or 0- and 8-week schedule. Primary endpoints were seroconversion rate (SR) difference and geometric mean titer (GMT) ratio at 42 days post-full immunization. Primary safety endpoint was incidence of adverse reaction (AR) within 28 days after either dose.

Serious adverse events were balanced (0.88% each), with no deaths or sequelae.

CONCLUSIONS: Test VarV demonstrated non-inferior immunogenicity and acceptable safety.

BACKGROUND: Clinical trial designs, eligibility criteria, and outcome measures in inherited bleeding disorders were developed around male phenotypes, creating evidence gaps for females. No prior effort has addressed these barriers.

OBJECTIVES: To examine barriers to female inclusion in bleeding disorders clinical research and propose strategies to modernize trial design.

METHODS: In October 2025, the National Bleeding Disorders Foundation convened a Research Roundtable of clinicians, researchers, industry representatives, and people with lived experience. Five sessions addressed key clinical and regulatory domains, informed by pre-meeting engagement with the FDA. Proceedings were synthesized into themes and consolidated into consensus recommendations.

FINDINGS: Females account for less than 1% of bleeding disorders clinical trial participants. Core barriers include male-derived severity constructs, bidirectional exclusion from reproductive health and haematology studies, impracticality of objective menstrual blood loss measurement, underuse of patient-reported outcomes, and fragile data infrastructure. A self-reinforcing cycle emerged in which sparse female enrolment prevents refinement of the constructs driving exclusion.

RECOMMENDATIONS: Four consensus recommendations were formulated: (1) validate multi-domain, female-relevant composite endpoints integrating biological markers, pragmatic bleeding measures, and patient-important outcomes; (2) engage regulators early to clarify evidentiary expectations for females; (3) leverage real-world evidence and patient registries to support endpoint development; and (4) advance coordinated, multi-stakeholder implementation with Lived Experience Experts as equal partners.

CONCLUSIONS: Modernizing inherited bleeding disorders research to include females is achievable using existing tools and infrastructure. This roadmap offers a practical pathway to close the evidence gap and advance health equity for females with bleeding disorders.

PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: Research on inherited bleeding disorders, such as hemophilia and Von Willebrand Disease has mostly been based on studies of males. Females with these conditions are almost never included in clinical trials, they make up less than 1 in 100 of all participants. This means healthcare providers have very little evidence to guide treatment decisions for females. There are several reasons for this gap. Clinical trials often use entry requirements based on male patterns of bleeding, such as joint bleeds or very low clotting factor levels. Many females experience different types of bleeding, including heavy periods, bleeding during pregnancy, and bleeding after childbirth, bleeding not measured in most studies. At the same time, studies focused on menstrual or pregnancy-related bleeding often exclude people with known bleeding disorders. This creates a double gap where females with bleeding disorders fall between two fields of research but are rarely included in either. The tools used to measure whether a treatment is working also do not capture what matters most to females, such as fatigue, pain, iron deficiency, and the impact of bleeding on daily life. In October 2025, the National Bleeding Disorders Foundation brought together doctors, researchers, industry partners, regulatory stakeholders, and people living with bleeding disorders to discuss how to fix this problem. The group identified key barriers and developed four main recommendations: •Working with females with bleeding disorders, create new ways to measure treatment success including female-specific bleeding, iron levels, fatigue, pain, and quality of life. •Work with regulatory agencies like the FDA early in the process to agree on what evidence is needed for females. •Use existing patient databases and registries to better understand bleeding patterns in females over time. •Build a coordinated effort across all stakeholders, including patients as equal partners, to put these changes into practice. The group acknowledged this work has limitations. The meeting was held in the United States and focused primarily on hemophilia and von Willebrand disease, so the recommendations may need to be adapted for other bleeding disorders and other countries. There were a finite number of participants. More voices must be included to capture the full breadth of opinions. The recommendations also still need to be tested and validated in real clinical trials. These steps aim to ensure future research includes females and produces the evidence needed to improve their care throughout their lives.

HIGHLIGHTS: Females account for less than 1% of clinical trial participants in inherited bleeding disorders research, driven by a self-reinforcing cycle in which male-derived severity constructs exclude female phenotypes, preventing the generation of evidence needed to reform those same constructs. Females are subject to bidirectional exclusion: bleeding disorder trials routinely exclude uterine bleeding outcomes, while reproductive health trials exclude individuals with underlying bleeding disorders. Validated, multi-dimensional composite endpoints integrating biological markers (e.g., iron kinetics), pragmatic bleeding measures, and patient-reported outcomes are needed to capture treatment benefit across the female lifespan. Early and iterative engagement with regulatory bodies, combined with strategic use of real-world data and patient registries, is essential to establish acceptable evidentiary pathways for female populations. Coordinated, multi-stakeholder implementation, with Lived Experience Experts as equal partners, is required to translate these consensus recommendations into inclusive clinical trial practice.

Effective immunosuppression is necessary for steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRPNS) treatment, but intravenous cyclophosphamide (CY) may impose treatment-related burdens. BACKGROUND: Health systems increasingly deploy predictive and generative artificial intelligence (AI) to improve care delivery and outcomes. Predictive AI models estimate the risk of future events to inform targeted interventions, while generative AI systems extract, summarize, and communicate information and may automate selected tasks. Routine deployment generates clinical, operational, and interaction data that can support evaluation of the effectiveness and safety of AI-enabled workflows.

METHODS: We review approaches to generating real-world evidence (RWE) from routine deployment of predictive and generative AI across four implementation configurations: silent deployment, human-in-the-loop (HITL), human-on-the-loop (HOTL), and automated. For both predictive and generative AI, silent deployment supports assessment of local performance and subgroup differences. Once predictive AI drives action, evaluating the effectiveness of an implemented workflow (HITL, HOTL, or automated) generally requires causal inference approaches. For generative AI, HITL implementations can generate data on edits, overrides, time on task, usability, cognitive burden, and safety-relevant failure modes. HOTL and automated generative AI implementations can generate data on escalation performance, human follow-up work, downstream outcomes, and safety signals.

CONCLUSION: AI implementation should be paired with methods for generating RWE on the effectiveness and safety of AI-enabled workflows. In HOTL and automated configurations, escalation performance and residual human workload become particularly important components of evaluation.
